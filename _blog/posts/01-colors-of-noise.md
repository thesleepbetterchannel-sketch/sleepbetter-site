If you've ever looked for something to fall asleep to, you've probably run into a whole rainbow of noise: white, pink, brown, green, even grey. It can sound like marketing. Mostly it isn't. Each color describes a real difference in how the sound is built, and once you know what to listen for, it's easier to pick the one that suits you and your bedroom.

Here's what each color actually is, what it sounds like, and what the research does (and doesn't) say about it.

## First, why "color"?

Every steady noise is a blend of frequencies, from low rumbles to high hiss. The "color" tells you how the energy is spread across that range. The name is borrowed from light: white light contains every color, and white noise contains every frequency. The other colors shift the balance toward the low end, the middle, or the way our ears actually hear.

## White noise: bright and even

White noise has equal energy at every frequency. Because there are far more high frequencies than low ones, and our ears are sensitive to them, white noise sounds bright and hissy, like TV static or a rushing air vent.

That breadth is its strength. It covers a wide range of sounds at once, which is why white noise machines have been around for decades. It's also the most studied color. In one small study, [10 New Yorkers with noisy bedrooms slept better with a white noise machine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34049045/), both by their own sleep diaries and by wrist-worn sleep trackers. It was a small study, and it was [partly funded by the machine's maker](https://yogasleep.com/pages/clinical-research-shows-white-noise-significantly-improves-sleep-quality-for-individuals-living-in-high-noise-environments), so it's a hint rather than proof.

**Listen:** [Fan Noise & Soft Rain, Cozy Bedroom](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v-u9Qk4h1Wk) (a box fan is a close cousin of white noise). Or try [Relaxing White Noise](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ofsNXre5Vq4), our softened, never-harsh white noise.

## Pink noise: softer, like steady rain

Pink noise turns the high end down. Its energy falls as the pitch rises (about 3 decibels per octave), so each octave carries the same amount of energy. The result is fuller and gentler than white noise, closer to steady rain or wind through trees.

Pink noise gets the most headlines, thanks to a [2017 Northwestern University study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5340797/). Researchers played short bursts of pink noise to 13 adults aged 60 and over during deep sleep, timed by a computer to match each person's brain waves. Their deep-sleep activity increased, and their overnight improvement on a memory test was about three times larger than on a night without the sound.

There's an important catch: those were carefully timed pulses, not a track playing all night. A [2026 University of Pennsylvania study](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/8452884) tested continuous pink noise instead. In 25 young adults in a sleep lab, pink noise at 50 decibels reduced REM sleep (the dreaming stage) by about 19 minutes, and it did little to protect sleep from simulated traffic noise. Foam earplugs worked much better. It's one study, but it's a good reason not to assume pink noise is automatically "better."

**Listen:** [Soft Pink Noise](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rtTsqzz2Svk)

## Brown noise: the deep rumble

Brown noise drops off even faster (about 6 decibels per octave), leaving mostly low tones. Think heavy rain on a roof, a distant waterfall, or the steady roar inside an airplane cabin. Despite the name, it has nothing to do with the color brown. It's named after the botanist Robert Brown and the random "Brownian" motion the sound's math is based on.

Brown noise went viral on social media, and plenty of people swear by it. To be honest about it, though, [researchers haven't specifically studied brown noise and sleep](https://www.sleep.com/sleep-health/brown-noise). Its appeal makes sense anyway: the low rumble is good at covering low sounds like traffic hum, footsteps upstairs or a furnace, and it has none of the hiss some people find tiring. We've written more about it in [why so many people fall asleep to brown noise](/blog/why-people-love-brown-noise/).

**Listen:** [Deep Brown Noise](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ug7TDj_AYmk) or [Brown Noise & Soft Rain](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h4LGSiSjjEI)

## Green noise: the middle ground

Green noise isn't a formal scientific term. It generally means noise centered on the middle frequencies, with the harsh highs and heavy lows pulled back. The [Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/noise-and-sleep/what-is-green-noise) describes it as sounding like ocean waves or a stream. There's no sleep research on green noise specifically, but many people find it a comfortable in-between: softer than white noise, lighter than brown.

**Listen:** [Soft Green Noise](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZDFy3eKCL2Q)

## Grey noise: shaped to your ears

Our ears don't hear all frequencies equally. We're most sensitive in the upper-middle range, around the shrill pitch of a smoke alarm. Grey noise is shaped to make up for that, turning down the frequencies we hear most easily and lifting the ones we don't, so every part of the sound *seems* equally loud.

Because hearing varies from person to person, there's no single "true" grey noise. It's used in hearing research and some tinnitus sound therapy, and it has a smooth, even, unobtrusive quality. Like green and brown noise, it hasn't been studied as a sleep aid.

**Listen:** [Soft Grey Noise](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f2mzUeqOVqA), a soft, even hush over a misty moonlit lake.

## So which color is best for sleep?

The honest answer: the science hasn't picked a winner. A [2021 review of 38 studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33007706/) from the University of Pennsylvania rated the evidence that continuous noise improves sleep as very low quality. That doesn't mean it can't help you. It means the studies so far have been small and inconsistent, and the results vary from person to person.

What steady sound does seem to do is *mask*. It's usually sudden changes that wake us: a car door, a dog, a creaky floor. A steady background makes those changes less noticeable. There's also habit. Playing the same sound every night can become a cue that it's time to sleep.

So choose by what you're trying to cover and what you find pleasant:

- **Voices, barking, sharper street sounds:** white or pink noise
- **Low rumbles (traffic, footsteps, furnaces), or you find hiss harsh:** brown noise
- **Something natural-feeling in between:** green noise
- **Every option feels like a bit much:** grey noise

Whichever you pick, give it a few nights before switching. Most sounds feel strange at first.

## Keep it low

More volume isn't better. Use the lowest level that covers the noise around you. It should sit in the background, not demand your attention.

This matters most for babies. A [2014 study in *Pediatrics*](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2013-3617) tested 14 infant sleep machines at full volume. All of them were louder than the recommended limit for hospital nurseries at 30 cm (about a foot), and three were louder than 85 decibels, the level the adult workplace limit allows for only an 8-hour shift. See our guide to [white noise for babies](/blog/white-noise-for-babies/) for safe use.

And if your main problem is outside noise, it's worth trying earplugs too. In the 2026 Penn study, they outperformed pink noise.

## When sound isn't enough

Sleep sounds are a comfort, not a cure. If you're struggling to sleep most nights for weeks at a time, snore loudly, wake up gasping, or feel exhausted during the day no matter how long you sleep, [talk to your doctor](/blog/when-to-see-a-doctor-about-sleep/). Those can be signs of something like insomnia or sleep apnea, and both are very treatable.
