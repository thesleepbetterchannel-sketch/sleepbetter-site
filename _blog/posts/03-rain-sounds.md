Ask people what sound helps them sleep, and rain comes up more than anything else. It isn't hard to see why. Rain is steady without being boring, soft without being silent, and it carries a feeling most of us know well: being warm and dry indoors while the weather does its thing outside.

There's some science behind that feeling, too.

## Rain is natural sound, and natural sound is calming

In a [2017 study at Brighton and Sussex Medical School](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5366899/), researchers scanned people's brains while they listened to recordings of natural and artificial environments. With natural sounds playing, the brain's resting networks shifted toward an outward, relaxed kind of attention, and the body showed more "rest and digest" nervous-system activity. With artificial sounds, the pattern looked more like the inward-focused attention seen in people who are anxious or stressed.

One detail stands out: [the people who were most stressed at the start showed the biggest relaxation response](https://bsms.ac.uk/about/news/2017/03-31-the-sound-of-nature-helps-us-relax.aspx). If you lie in bed with a racing mind, that's exactly the situation where a natural soundscape seems to help most.

The study didn't measure sleep itself, so it doesn't prove rain makes you sleep better. But relaxing is the first step toward falling asleep, and rain appears to help with that.

## Rain covers the sounds that wake you

The other thing rain does well is mask. It's usually not noise itself that wakes us, but sudden changes: a car door, a dog barking, a creak in the hallway. Research on night-time noise shows that [individual noise events can trigger brief arousals even at fairly low volumes](https://research-collection.ethz.ch/handle/20.500.11850/59871?show=full), whether they come from traffic, neighbors or church bells.

A steady wash of rain raises the background level of the room, so those sudden sounds stand out less. Rain also spans a wide range of pitches, from the low drum on a roof to the high patter on glass, so it covers a lot of different noises at once.

## Rain isn't one sound

Different recordings suit different rooms and different people:

- **Rain on a tin roof** is heavier and lower. It's good for covering traffic and household rumbles.
- **Rain on glass** is lighter and brighter, with more detail. It suits quieter rooms and people who find heavy rain too intense.
- **Soft rain in a forest** is gentle and diffuse, with very few sharp edges.
- **Rain with thunder** is cozy for some people and jarring for others. If you're a light sleeper, start without it.

The best one is simply the one you find pleasant. A sound you like will relax you. A sound that irritates you, however "scientific," won't.

## How to listen

- **Keep the volume low,** just enough to soften the sounds around you. The [NIDCD](https://noisyplanet.nidcd.nih.gov/parents/too-loud-too-long) considers 70 decibels and below safe for long listening, and your sleep sound should sit well under that.
- **Use a long, steady track.** A recording that loops every few minutes, or ends at 3 a.m., can pull you back out of sleep.
- **Keep the screen dark.** Put your phone face down or turn the TV's picture off. Light during sleep has its own costs.
