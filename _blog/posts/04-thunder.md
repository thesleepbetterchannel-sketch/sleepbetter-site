There are two kinds of people during a thunderstorm. One kind pulls the blanket up and sleeps better than ever. The other wakes at every crack and lies there counting the seconds to the next one. Both reactions make sense, and the difference comes down to how a sleeping brain treats sudden sound.

## Your brain keeps listening while you sleep

Sleep isn't an off switch for your ears. Throughout the night your brain keeps checking the sounds around you, and it's especially alert to changes. Lab research on traffic noise shows that [individual noise events trigger brief arousals at fairly low levels, regardless of where the noise comes from](https://research-collection.ethz.ch/handle/20.500.11850/59871?show=full).

Many of those arousals never fully wake you. In one sleep-lab study, [traffic noises that didn't wake people still pushed their heart rate up by about 9 beats per minute](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2279756/) before it settled back down. The researchers found that the size of the response depended on how loud the sound was, how quickly it rose, and which stage of sleep the person was in.

Thunder is close to a worst case on all three counts: it can be loud, it often arrives suddenly, and it comes at random.

## So why do some people love it?

Context matters a great deal. A storm heard from a warm bed can feel safe and enclosing, especially with steady rain underneath it. The rain raises the background level of the room, so each rumble stands out less against it. Rolling, distant thunder is also very different from a sharp crack overhead. It builds slowly, which gives the brain less of a jolt.

Personal history plays a part too. If storms meant cozy nights at a grandparent's house, your brain may file thunder as comforting. If a storm once took the power out or damaged your home, it may file it as a threat.

## Getting the cozy without the jolts

- **Choose rolling, distant thunder** over sharp, close cracks.
- **Make sure there's steady rain underneath.** The constant rain sound does most of the masking work.
- **Keep the volume modest.** Louder storms mean bigger peaks, and peaks are what disturb sleep. Long, loud listening also adds up for your hearing: the [NIDCD](https://noisyplanet.nidcd.nih.gov/parents/too-loud-too-long) treats 70 decibels and below as safe over long periods.
- **Try it at bedtime first.** If you enjoy thunder while you're drifting off but find it wakes you at 2 a.m., a rain-only track or a sleep timer may suit you better.
- **Light sleepers, start with plain rain.** You can always add thunder later.
