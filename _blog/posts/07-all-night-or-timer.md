It's one of the most common questions about sleep sounds: once you've fallen asleep, should the sound keep going? There's no single right answer, but there is a simple way to work out which one is right for you.

## Start with why you're using sound

People play sleep sounds for two different reasons, and they lead to different answers.

**To fall asleep.** If the sound mostly helps you relax, stop your thoughts from racing, or signal that it's bedtime, you may not need it once you're out. A timer of 30 to 60 minutes covers the time it takes most people to drift off.

**To stay asleep.** If you're using sound to cover noise, like traffic, a snoring partner, a neighbor's TV or a dog down the street, then it needs to last as long as the noise does. Noise doesn't stop being disruptive once you're asleep. Research shows that [individual noise events can cause brief arousals at relatively low levels](https://research-collection.ethz.ch/handle/20.500.11850/59871?show=full), often without fully waking you.

A sudden silence can be its own problem, too. If a track ends at 3 a.m., the room's background level drops at once, and every creak and car becomes easier to hear.

## What the research says about all-night sound

Here's the honest picture. A [2021 review of 38 studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33007706/) found the evidence that continuous noise improves sleep is very low quality. The authors also noted that all-night noise could have downsides for sleep and hearing, and called for better research before it's widely promoted.

A [2026 sleep-lab study](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/8452884) from the same University of Pennsylvania team added a useful data point. In 25 healthy young adults, continuous pink noise at 50 decibels reduced REM sleep by about 19 minutes compared with a quiet night. Foam earplugs were better than pink noise at protecting sleep from traffic sounds.

That's one small study in a lab, with people who weren't used to sleeping with sound. It doesn't mean your rain track is harming you. It's a reason to keep the volume low, and to use all-night sound when you actually need it rather than by default.

## A simple way to decide

- **Quiet bedroom, sound just helps you relax?** Use a timer.
- **Noisy bedroom or noisy household?** Let it run all night, at the lowest volume that does the job. Consider earplugs too.
- **Waking up when the sound stops?** That's a sign you need it all night, or a longer track.
- **Not sure?** Try a week each way and notice how you feel in the morning.

## Whatever you choose, keep it low

Volume matters more than timing. The [NIDCD](https://noisyplanet.nidcd.nih.gov/parents/too-loud-too-long) considers sounds at or below 70 decibels safe even over long periods, and says long exposure at 85 decibels or more can damage hearing. A sleep sound should be far quieter than either. Put the speaker across the room rather than beside your pillow, and if you use headphones, pick comfortable ones and keep the volume very low.
