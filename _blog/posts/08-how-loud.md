When the neighbors are loud, it's tempting to turn the rain up until you can't hear them. But for sleep sounds, more volume is rarely better, and over a whole night it can work against you.

## What counts as safe

The National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders (NIDCD) gives a clear guide. [Sounds at or below 70 A-weighted decibels (dBA) are generally considered safe, even over long periods.](https://noisyplanet.nidcd.nih.gov/parents/too-loud-too-long) Sounds at 85 dBA or above are more likely to damage hearing over time, and the louder the sound, the faster that damage can happen.

For a sense of scale, the NIDCD puts [normal conversation at around 60 to 70 dBA](https://hearingreview.com/inside-hearing/events/nidcd-announces-public-education-campaign-for-national-protect-your-hearing-month). A sleep sound should be quieter than that. Something closer to a soft hum in the background is the goal.

## Quieter is often better for sleep, too

Hearing safety isn't the only reason to keep it down.

- **Masking doesn't need much volume.** The aim isn't to drown out the noise around you, just to raise the background enough that sudden sounds stand out less.
- **Loud sound has peaks.** Even steady recordings like rain or waves have small swells and pops. At high volume, those can disturb you.
- **Research urges caution.** A [2021 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33007706/) noted that all-night noise may affect both sleep and hearing, and a [2026 lab study](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/8452884) found that steady pink noise played at 50 dBA reduced REM sleep in young adults. That's a fairly quiet level, which is a good reason to stay modest.

## How to find your level

You don't need a sound meter. Try this:

1. **Start with the sound off** and listen to your room for a minute. Notice what's bothering you.
2. **Turn the sound on very low** and raise it slowly, just until the bothersome noise blends in and stops grabbing your attention.
3. **Stop there.** It should feel like part of the room, not something you're listening to.
4. **Place the speaker across the room,** not by your pillow. Distance makes a big difference to how loud a sound is at your ear.

If you can't get the noise to blend in at a comfortable level, sound may not be the right tool on its own. Foam earplugs, heavier curtains or moving the bed away from a shared wall can help more.

## A word on headphones and earbuds

Sleep headphones can be handy if you share a room. Keep the volume very low, because the speaker sits right at your ear. The [Hearing Health Foundation](https://hearinghealthfoundation.org/decibel-levels) suggests keeping headphones and earbuds at no more than 50 to 60 percent of their maximum volume, and for sleep you'll likely want far less than that.

## Babies and children

Little ears need extra care. If you use a sound machine in a nursery, keep it low and well away from the crib. We've written a separate guide on [white noise for babies](/blog/white-noise-for-babies/).
