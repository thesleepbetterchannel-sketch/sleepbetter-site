Most of us go from full speed to lights-out with nothing in between: answer one more email, finish the episode, then expect sleep to arrive on cue. It rarely works that way. Sleep comes more easily when you give it a runway.

## Why routines work

There's a reason the CDC's first tip for better sleep is about consistency: [go to bed at the same time each night and get up at the same time each morning, including on weekends](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about_sleep/sleep_hygiene.html). Your body clock runs on regular signals. The more predictable your evenings are, the more reliably your body starts getting sleepy at the right time.

A routine also works by association. When you do the same few things in the same order every night, they become a cue. Sleep therapists use this idea on purpose. A core part of the most effective insomnia treatment, called stimulus control, is [strengthening the link between your bed and sleep](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853211/) and breaking the link between your bed and lying awake.

## Build yours in four parts

You don't need a complicated ritual. Thirty to sixty minutes, with these four pieces, is plenty.

**1. A clear starting signal.** Pick a set time, or a set action, that starts the wind-down: dimming the lights, putting the phone on its charger in another room, or turning on your sleep sound. Set a reminder if bedtime tends to slip.

**2. Lower the light.** Switch to lamps instead of ceiling lights, and turn screens down or off. Bright evening light [delays your body clock and holds back melatonin](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25535358/), the hormone that helps you feel sleepy.

**3. Something calm you enjoy.** Reading a paper book, gentle stretching, a warm drink without caffeine, a bath, journaling, or quiet music. It should feel like a pleasure, not a chore, or you won't keep it up.

**4. The same last step every night.** Brush your teeth, set out tomorrow's clothes, start the rain, lights out. Repeating the same final steps is what turns them into a signal.

## Clear your head before bed

If worries follow you to bed, give them somewhere to go earlier in the evening. Spend five minutes writing tomorrow's to-do list, or jot down whatever is on your mind. It's easier to let a thought go once it's on paper.

## What to leave out

The CDC recommends [avoiding large meals, caffeine and alcohol before bedtime](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about_sleep/sleep_hygiene.html) and keeping TVs, computers and phones out of the bedroom. Work emails, the news and anything else that gets your heart rate up are best saved for the morning.

## Making it stick

- **Start small.** One or two new habits are easier to keep than ten.
- **Keep it portable** so you can do it while traveling.
- **Be flexible on the details, firm on the shape.** A late night out doesn't ruin anything. A shortened ten-minute version still counts.
- **Give it two weeks.** Routines take time to become automatic.
