As the clocks go back and the days shorten, a lot of people notice their sleep changing. Getting up in the dark is harder, afternoons feel heavy, and some people want to sleep far more than usual. Others find their bedtime drifting later. Here's what's going on, and what helps.

## Why the season affects your sleep

Your body clock relies on light, especially morning light, to stay in step with the day. In winter there's less of it, it arrives later, and many of us spend daylight hours indoors. With a weaker signal, your clock can drift, leaving you sleepy when you want to be alert and alert when you want to sleep.

Melatonin, the hormone that rises in darkness to help bring on sleep, is involved too. The National Institute of Mental Health explains that people with winter-pattern seasonal affective disorder [produce too much melatonin, which can increase sleepiness and lead to oversleeping](https://infocenter.nimh.nih.gov/sites/default/files/publications/seasonal-affective-disorder.pdf).

## What helps

**Get morning light, every day.** This is the single most useful habit for winter. Go outside within an hour or two of waking, even when it's gray. Daylight on a cloudy morning is still much brighter than indoor lighting. Walk to work, have coffee on the porch, or sit by the brightest window you have.

**Keep a steady wake-up time.** The CDC's top sleep tip, [the same bedtime and wake-up time every day, including weekends](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/about_sleep/sleep_hygiene.html), matters even more when daylight is weak. A consistent alarm gives your body clock something to hold on to.

**Resist the urge to hibernate.** Long lie-ins and late-afternoon naps can feel wonderful but tend to make the drift worse. Keep naps short and early.

**Stay active.** Regular exercise, ideally outdoors in daylight, supports both mood and sleep.

**Make evenings cozy, not bright.** Lean into winter: lamps instead of overhead lights, a warm drink without caffeine, a crackling fire, a soft snowfall on the speaker. Dim, warm evenings help your body get ready for sleep.

**Watch the heating.** Cozy evenings are great, but a bedroom that's too warm can break up your sleep. Many sleep specialists suggest a [bedroom around 60 to 67°F](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/what-is-the-ideal-sleeping-temperature-for-my-bedroom).

## When it's more than the winter blues

For some people, winter brings more than sluggishness. Seasonal affective disorder (SAD) is a type of depression that comes and goes with the seasons. The NIMH describes symptoms that [last about four to five months a year](https://infocenter.nimh.nih.gov/sites/default/files/publications/seasonal-affective-disorder.pdf) and can include feeling down most of the day, losing interest in things you enjoy, changes in appetite or weight, sleep problems, and low energy.

SAD is treatable. Light therapy, using a special bright light box each day, has been a [mainstay of treatment since the 1980s](https://infocenter.nimh.nih.gov/sites/default/files/publications/seasonal-affective-disorder.pdf), and talk therapy and medication can also help. People with some eye conditions or who take medicines that increase light sensitivity may need medical guidance first. If this sounds like you, talk to your doctor rather than going it alone.
