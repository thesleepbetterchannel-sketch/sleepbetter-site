We make sleep sounds, and we love hearing that they help. But it would be wrong to suggest that rain on a tin roof can fix every sleep problem. Some sleep problems are medical conditions that need proper diagnosis and treatment. The good news is that the most common ones are very treatable.

## Signs of sleep apnea

Obstructive sleep apnea happens when the airway narrows or closes during sleep, so breathing repeatedly stops and starts. Many people who have it don't know, because it happens while they're asleep. A bed partner is often the first to notice.

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine lists these [signs to watch for](https://sleepeducation.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/COS-Public-Handout.pdf).

**While sleeping:**
- Snoring or noisy breathing
- Pauses in breathing
- Choking or gasping sounds
- Restless sleep or insomnia
- Waking often to use the bathroom

**While awake:**
- Daytime sleepiness or fatigue
- Morning headaches
- Trouble focusing or remembering
- Irritability or low mood
- Feeling drowsy while driving

Not everyone who snores has sleep apnea. But [gasping or choking during sleep is one of the most important signs](https://sleepapnea.org/symptoms/) that someone should be referred for a sleep study. Left untreated, sleep apnea is linked to problems with heart health and diabetes, and to slower reaction times. Diagnosis is usually done with a sleep study, often at home now, and treatment works well.

## Signs of chronic insomnia

Everyone has a bad night now and then. Insomnia becomes chronic when trouble falling or staying asleep [happens at least three nights a week for at least three months](https://www.acponline.org/node/296994) and causes real distress or affects how you function during the day.

If that's you, there's an effective treatment that doesn't involve pills. The American College of Physicians recommends [cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) as the first treatment](https://www.acponline.org/acp-newsroom/acp-recommends-cognitive-behavioral-therapy-as-initial-treatment-forchronic-insomnia) for adults with chronic insomnia. It combines changes to sleep habits and schedules with techniques for the worries and thoughts that keep people awake, and it can be done in person, in groups, by phone, online or through self-help books.

## Other reasons to check in

- **Persistent daytime sleepiness,** like nodding off while reading, watching TV or in meetings, even after a full night in bed.
- **An urge to move your legs** in the evening, often with uncomfortable feelings that ease when you move.
- **Acting out dreams,** such as shouting, punching or kicking in your sleep.
- **Sleep that changes suddenly** for no clear reason, or alongside low mood or anxiety.
- **Relying on alcohol or sleep aids** to get to sleep most nights.

## How to bring it up

The AASM suggests a few simple steps:

- **Be specific** about your symptoms, both at night and during the day, and how often they happen.
- **Bring notes from your partner** or anyone who's watched you sleep.
- **Ask directly** whether your symptoms could be signs of sleep apnea or another sleep disorder.

A week or two of simple sleep notes before your appointment helps too: when you went to bed, roughly when you fell asleep, how often you woke, and how you felt the next day.

## Drowsy driving

If you're ever fighting to stay awake behind the wheel, pull over somewhere safe. Drowsy driving is a symptom worth taking seriously and a good reason to book that appointment.
