How Alcohol Affects Your Sleep

A nightcap can help you fall asleep faster, but a review of the research found it disrupts the second half of the night. Here's what's going on, and how to drink in a way that's kinder to your sleep.

November 6, 2026 · 3 min read

A drink before bed feels like it helps. You get drowsy, you drift off quickly, and you sleep heavily for a while. The trouble starts later, usually in the early hours, when you're less likely to notice what's happening.

What the research found

In 2013, researchers at The London Sleep Centre reviewed the sleep-lab research on alcohol in healthy adults. Looking at 20 studies with more than 500 people, they found a consistent pattern:

  • Alcohol helps you fall asleep faster, at any amount.
  • It deepens sleep in the first half of the night.
  • It disrupts sleep in the second half, with more waking as the alcohol wears off.
  • At moderate and higher amounts, it reduces REM sleep, the dreaming stage that matters for memory and mood. Moderate (2 to 4 drinks) and high doses reduced overall REM sleep across the night.

The lead author's summary was blunt: on the whole, alcohol isn't useful for improving a whole night's sleep. Sleep may start deeper, but it becomes disrupted.

Other ways alcohol affects sleep

  • Breathing. Alcohol relaxes the muscles of the throat. The lead researcher noted that with increasing amounts, it suppresses breathing and can turn non-snorers into snorers, and snorers into people with sleep apnea.
  • Bathroom trips. Alcohol makes you need to pee, which can wake you in the night.
  • Habit. Using alcohol to fall asleep regularly can lead to relying on it, and it's not considered an appropriate sleep aid.

How much is too much?

The same review found that one or two standard drinks seem to have only small effects on sleep for most people, while the disruption grows with each drink after that. Everyone is different, though, and the effect also depends on timing.

Kinder ways to drink

  • Give yourself time. The lead researcher suggested leaving an hour and a half to two hours between your last drink and bed, so the alcohol is already wearing off. More time is better after larger amounts.
  • Keep it to one or two on nights when sleep matters most, like before an early start.
  • Don't use it to fall asleep. If you find you need a drink to sleep, it's worth talking to your doctor about what's really keeping you up.

A better nightcap

If you like a ritual before bed, swap the drink for something warm and caffeine-free, like herbal tea or warm milk. Pair it with dim lights and a sleep sound, and you get the wind-down without the 3 a.m. wake-up.

Sources

  • Ebrahim IO, Shapiro CM, Williams AJ, Fenwick PB. Alcohol and sleep I: effects on normal sleep. Alcoholism: Clinical & Experimental Research, 2013. Summaries: Live Science via Fox News, NBC News, WebMD via Park Street, Aju Daily.
