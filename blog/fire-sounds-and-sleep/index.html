Crackling Fire Sounds and Why "Cozy" Helps You Sleep

A University of Alabama study found that watching a fire lowered blood pressure, but only when the crackle could be heard. Here's what that means for bedtime.

November 6, 2026 · 3 min read

There's a reason fireplace videos rack up millions of views every winter. The soft pop and hiss of burning wood seems to slow people down in a way few other sounds do. It turns out the crackle may matter more than the flames.

The study that tested campfire calm

Anthropologist Christopher Lynn at the University of Alabama wanted to know whether the old idea that fires are relaxing actually holds up. In a set of three studies with 226 adults, he measured blood pressure before and after people watched a video of a fire with sound, the same fire with the sound muted, or control screens.

The results were consistent: blood pressure dropped in the fire-with-sound condition, and it dropped more the longer people watched. The silent fire didn't produce the same effect. In the university's summary, the muted fire and an upside-down picture of a fire actually seemed to agitate people rather than calm them.

Lynn's conclusion was that fireside relaxation is a multisensory experience. The light, the warmth and the sound work together, and the sound is a big part of it.

What that means for sleep

The study measured relaxation, not sleep, so it doesn't prove that fire sounds help you sleep longer or more deeply. But winding down is the step before falling asleep, and anything that genuinely lowers your arousal at bedtime is working in your favor.

Fire sounds have a couple of other things going for them:

  • They're steady with gentle variety. A good fire has a constant soft roar with small, irregular pops on top. That keeps the ear lightly engaged without demanding attention.
  • They carry associations. For many people, fire means cabins, holidays and evenings with nowhere to be. Those feelings can do a lot to quiet a busy mind.

Fire plus rain

Our most popular combination is a crackling fireplace with rain falling outside. The rain adds a steady broadband layer that helps cover household noise, while the fire gives the sound warmth and texture. If you find fire alone a little too sparse, or rain alone a little too cold, try the two together.

Tips for listening

  • Listen before bed as well as during sleep. The study found the calming effect grew with time, so starting the sound while you read or wind down may help.
  • Keep the screen dark. Fire videos are lovely to watch, but light during sleep has its own downsides. Watch for a few minutes, then put the screen face down or turn the picture off.
  • Keep it quiet. The pops in a fire recording are small peaks. At low volume they're soothing. At high volume they can startle.

Sources
