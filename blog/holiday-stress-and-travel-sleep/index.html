Sleeping Well Through Holiday Stress and Travel

Late nights, rich food, extra drinks, guest rooms and time zones. The holidays are hard on sleep. Here's how to protect it without missing the fun, with tips from the CDC's travel health guide.

November 6, 2026 · 3 min read

The holidays pile up nearly everything that disrupts sleep: late nights, big meals, more alcohol, unfamiliar beds, busy houses, long drives and flights across time zones, plus the stress of hosting or being hosted. You don't have to give up the fun to sleep well, but a little planning goes a long way.

Keep your anchor

If you protect only one thing, make it a steady wake-up time. Late nights are part of the season, but sleeping until noon the next day pushes your body clock later and makes the following night harder. Try to get up within an hour or so of your usual time, get some daylight, and take a short early-afternoon nap if you need it.

Go easy on the nightcap

Holiday drinks can feel like they help you sleep, but a review of the research found alcohol deepens sleep early in the night and disrupts it in the second half, with less REM sleep at moderate and higher amounts. Alternating with water and finishing your last drink a couple of hours before bed both help.

Large late dinners can also keep you up. The CDC suggests avoiding large meals, caffeine and alcohol before bedtime. Holiday coffee counts too. A study from Henry Ford Hospital found that caffeine taken even six hours before bed cut sleep by more than an hour.

Sleeping in someone else's house

The first night in a new place is often a poor one. Brown University researchers found that part of the brain stays on watch on the first night somewhere unfamiliar. To help it settle:

  • Bring your usual sleep sound, downloaded in advance, and earplugs and an eye mask.
  • Ask to turn the heat down in your room, or crack a window.
  • Keep your own wind-down: same order, same last steps.

Crossing time zones

Jet lag happens when your body clock is out of step with local time, and it typically shows up after crossing two or more time zones. The CDC's travel health guide, the Yellow Book, recommends:

  • Using light as your main tool. Timed light exposure is a standard treatment, and the guide recommends maximizing natural light at your destination. In general, morning light helps after flying east and evening light helps after flying west.
  • Staying hydrated and avoiding too much alcohol on the flight.
  • Short naps only. Naps of 20 to 30 minutes can keep you going during the local day, while longer naps may interfere with sleep that night.
  • Choosing flights wisely. Pick an arrival time that lets you get daylight at the right part of the day.

For a short trip of a day or two, it may be easier to stay on home time as much as possible.

Holiday stress

Busy minds make for restless nights. Give your worries somewhere to go before bed: a to-do list for tomorrow, a few minutes of quiet, a warm drink, low lights. If you're hosting, build a short break into the evening for yourself.

If you wake at 3 a.m. with your mind racing, don't lie there fighting it. Our guide to getting back to sleep explains what sleep therapists recommend.
