Naps: How Long Is Too Long?

A short nap can lift your afternoon. A long one can leave you groggy and wreck your bedtime. Here's how long to nap, when, and who should skip it.

November 6, 2026 · 2 min read

A good nap is one of life's small pleasures. A bad one leaves you groggier than before and lying awake at midnight. The difference is mostly about length and timing.

The sweet spot: about 20 minutes

Most sleep experts land in the same place. Cleveland Clinic says a 15- to 20-minute power nap is best for a boost in alertness, and warns against napping longer than an hour. The CDC's travel health guidance, written by sleep researchers, says short daytime naps of 20 to 30 minutes can help sustain alertness, while longer naps may interfere with sleep that night.

Why longer naps backfire

Sleep comes in stages. In the first stretch of a nap you're in light sleep, and waking from it is easy. Go much past half an hour and you can slide into deep sleep. Waking from deep sleep brings on sleep inertia, that heavy, foggy, "where am I" feeling that can take a while to shake off.

Long naps also use up some of your sleep drive, the pressure to sleep that builds up across the day. Spend it in the afternoon and there's less left for bedtime.

When to nap

  • Early to mid-afternoon is the natural window. Many people feel a dip in alertness after lunch.
  • Avoid late-day naps. Napping in the early evening is the most likely to push your bedtime later.

How to take a good nap

  1. Set an alarm for 20 to 25 minutes, so you have a few minutes to drift off.
  2. Lie down somewhere dim and quiet, or use an eye mask.
  3. Use a sleep sound to cover household or office noise.
  4. Give yourself a few minutes after waking before doing anything demanding.

If you can't fall asleep, that's fine. Resting quietly with your eyes closed still helps.

When you might skip naps

If you have trouble sleeping at night, naps can make it worse. Many insomnia programs recommend avoiding daytime naps while you rebuild a steady night-time pattern, because naps reduce the sleep pressure that helps you fall asleep and stay asleep.

And if you find you need a nap every day just to function, it may be a sign you're not getting enough sleep at night, or that something is disturbing your sleep. Regular, heavy daytime sleepiness is worth mentioning to your doctor. Our guide on when to talk to a doctor about sleep explains what to look for.
