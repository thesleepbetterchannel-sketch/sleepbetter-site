How Much Sleep Do Adults Actually Need?

The short answer is seven hours or more. Here's where that number comes from, why about a third of American adults fall short, and how to tell if you're getting enough.

November 6, 2026 · 3 min read

The short answer: most adults need at least seven hours of sleep a night. Here's where that number comes from, and how to work out what it means for you.

Where "seven or more" comes from

In 2015, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society brought together a panel of experts to review the research on sleep and health. Their joint statement recommends that adults aged 18 to 60 sleep at least 7 hours each night for the best health and well-being. The CDC uses the same benchmark, defining healthy sleep for adults as 7 or more hours a day.

For older adults, the National Institute on Aging says the need doesn't really shrink: older adults need about the same amount of sleep as all adults, 7 to 9 hours. What changes with age is usually how easy it is to get.

Most of us fall short

When the CDC surveyed more than 440,000 adults across every state, about 35 percent said they usually got less than 7 hours. The share varied a lot by state, from about 56 percent getting enough sleep in Hawaii to 72 percent in South Dakota.

Short sleep isn't just about feeling groggy. CDC data show that adults who sleep less than 7 hours are more likely to report conditions like heart disease, diabetes, obesity and depression. Those are links, not proof that short sleep causes each one, but the pattern is consistent across many studies.

"Seven or more" is a floor, not a target

Some people need eight or nine hours to feel their best. A few signs you may need more than you're getting:

  • You rely on an alarm every day and still feel like you're being dragged awake.
  • You sleep much longer on days off.
  • You feel drowsy in quiet moments: reading, in meetings, as a passenger in a car.
  • You need caffeine to get through the afternoon.

A simple test is a week off with no alarm. After the first few days of catching up, the amount you settle into naturally is a good estimate of your real need.

Quality counts too

Hours in bed aren't the same as hours asleep. If you're getting seven or eight hours and still wake up tired, something may be breaking up your sleep: noise, light, alcohol, an uneven schedule, or a sleep disorder like sleep apnea. Our guide on when to talk to a doctor about sleep covers the signs worth checking.

Getting closer to seven

  • Pick a steady wake-up time and count back seven to eight hours to find your bedtime. Consistency matters as much as length.
  • Protect the hour before bed. Dim the lights and start winding down.
  • Treat sleep like an appointment. If bedtime keeps slipping, set a reminder to start getting ready.
