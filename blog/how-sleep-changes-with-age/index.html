How Sleep Changes as We Get Older

Older adults need about as much sleep as younger ones, but sleep often becomes lighter, earlier and more broken. Here's what's normal, what isn't, and what helps, according to the National Institute on Aging.

November 6, 2026 · 3 min read

A lot of people assume that needing less sleep is just part of getting older. It isn't. What changes is usually how easily sleep comes, not how much of it you need.

How much sleep older adults need

According to the National Institute on Aging (NIA), older adults need about the same amount of sleep as all adults: 7 to 9 hours each night.

What tends to change

The NIA describes several common shifts. Older adults tend to go to bed earlier and wake up earlier than they did when younger. Sleep tends to be shorter and lighter, with less time in each of the deeper stages, and people wake more often during the night. Older adults are also more likely to take medications and have health conditions that affect sleep.

Some of this is a normal part of aging. But a lot of poor sleep in later life has causes that can be treated.

Common causes of poor sleep in later life

The NIA points to illness, pain, medications and worries as frequent reasons older adults don't sleep well. Some specific ones:

  • Insomnia. The NIA notes that insomnia is the most common sleep problem in adults age 60 and older. The recommended first treatment for chronic insomnia is cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), and the American College of Physicians found it reduced time spent awake during the night in older adults.
  • Sleep apnea, where breathing repeatedly stops and starts during sleep. Loud snoring and gasping are key signs.
  • Movement disorders. Restless legs syndrome and other movement disorders that can rob you of sleep become more common with age.
  • Night-time bathroom trips, pain from arthritis, and side effects from medicines.

What helps

The NIA's tips for a good night's sleep include:

  • Follow a regular schedule. Go to bed and get up at the same times, even when retired.
  • Get daylight. Time outdoors, especially in the morning, helps keep your body clock steady.
  • Stay active, but not right before bed.
  • Be careful with long or late naps, which can make night-time sleep harder.
  • Limit caffeine later in the day and remember that alcohol won't help you sleep. Even small amounts make it harder to stay asleep.
  • Cut back on drinks in the evening to reduce trips to the bathroom.
  • Ask about your medicines. Some can affect sleep, and timing can sometimes be changed.

A low, steady background sound can help too, especially if you've become a lighter sleeper who wakes to small noises in the house.

Sleep aids: be cautious

Some older adults turn to over-the-counter or prescription sleep aids. These can have side effects and interact with other medicines, and the American College of Physicians recommends trying CBT-I before medication for chronic insomnia. Talk to your doctor before starting any sleep medicine, and ask about CBT-I as a first step.

When to talk to a doctor

The NIA advises that waking up every day feeling tired is a sign you're not getting the rest you need. If that's you, or if a partner notices loud snoring or pauses in your breathing, it's worth a visit. Our guide on when to see a doctor about sleep has more.
