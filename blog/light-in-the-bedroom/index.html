Light in the Bedroom: Blackout Curtains, Night Lights and Morning Sun

Light is the strongest signal your body clock gets. A Northwestern study found even a single night of moderate room light during sleep raised heart rate and insulin resistance. Here's how to get light right, day and night.

November 6, 2026 · 3 min read

Light is the main signal your body clock uses to tell day from night. Getting the right light at the right time is one of the simplest, most effective things you can do for your sleep. Think of it as two jobs: keep the night dark, and make the morning bright.

Keep the night dark

In a 2022 Northwestern University study, healthy young adults spent one night sleeping in a dimly lit room and another in moderate room light, about what you'd get from overhead lights left on. After the brighter night:

  • their heart rate stayed higher during sleep,
  • their nervous system showed signs of being more "switched on" when it should have been resting,
  • and the next morning their bodies showed higher insulin resistance, a risk factor for diabetes.

Participants spent more of the night in lighter sleep and less in deep and REM sleep in the lit room, even though their eyes were closed. As one of the researchers put it, the brain senses the light even when you don't.

This was a small study of 20 people over single nights, so it doesn't tell us about the long-term effects of a night light. But it's a good reason to aim for a properly dark bedroom.

How to darken your room

  • Blackout curtains or blinds for streetlights and early summer sunrises.
  • An eye mask if you can't control the light, such as when traveling or sharing a room.
  • Cover or switch off small lights. Chargers, TVs, smoke alarms and power strips all glow. A bit of electrical tape works wonders.
  • Turn the TV picture off. If you fall asleep to a video, set the screen to turn off or put the phone face down. Your ears can keep listening while your eyes get darkness.
  • Need a night light? Choose a dim, warm-colored one placed low, rather than a bright white light. A Koç University summary of the study suggests keeping any light you need dim and amber or red rather than white or blue.

Make the morning bright

Darkness at night is only half the story. Bright light in the morning helps set your body clock so you get sleepy at the right time in the evening. The CDC's travel guidance uses this directly, recommending that travelers get as much natural light as possible after arriving to help their body clocks adjust.

  • Get outside soon after you wake, even for a few minutes. Daylight, even on cloudy days, is far brighter than indoor light.
  • Open the curtains as soon as you're up.
  • Have breakfast by a window.

And dim the evening

Bright light late in the evening pushes your clock in the wrong direction. A Harvard-led study found that reading on a bright tablet before bed held back melatonin and shifted the body clock later. Switch to lamps in the last hour or two before bed and keep screens dim.
