Sleeping Somewhere New: How to Use Sound in Hotels and While Traveling

There's a reason the first night in a hotel is often the worst. Brown University researchers found half your brain stays on watch. Here's how to help it settle.

November 6, 2026 · 3 min read

If you've ever slept badly on the first night of a trip, there's a name for it: the first-night effect. Sleep researchers have known about it for decades. It's so common that sleep labs often throw out the data from a person's first night. A study from Brown University helped explain why it happens.

Half your brain keeps watch

In a 2016 study in Current Biology, researchers measured the brain activity of 35 volunteers over two nights in a sleep lab, a week apart. On the first night, a network in the left side of the brain stayed noticeably more alert than the right during deep sleep. On the second night, the difference disappeared.

The more watchful side also responded more strongly to unusual sounds, and people woke up more often, and faster, when those sounds were played into the ear that side of the brain listens to. The researchers suggest it's a kind of night watch: an old survival instinct that keeps part of the brain on guard in an unfamiliar place.

In other words, it's not you. Your brain is doing what it was built to do.

How sound can help

Sound can help in two ways.

It blocks the unfamiliar noises. Hotel corridors, ice machines, elevators, unfamiliar traffic and a strange air conditioner are exactly the kind of new, irregular sounds the watchful brain reacts to. A steady sound raises the background level so those sounds stand out less.

It brings something familiar with you. One of the study's authors suggested bringing something that makes a new place feel comfortable, like your own pillow. The sound you fall asleep to at home can do the same job. If you play the same rain or brown noise every night, it becomes part of what "bedtime" feels like, wherever you are.

A travel sleep kit

  • Your usual sleep sound, downloaded ahead of time in case the Wi-Fi is bad. Choose a long track so it doesn't stop in the middle of the night.
  • Foam earplugs. In a 2026 sleep-lab study, earplugs protected sleep from traffic noise better than pink noise did.
  • An eye mask, for curtains that don't quite close and light under the door.
  • Your pillowcase, or anything else that smells and feels like home.

Tips for your first night

  • Set up the room before bed. Turn the thermostat down a little, close the curtains fully, and check for blinking lights on TVs and smoke alarms.
  • Put the phone across the room with the sound playing, face down.
  • Keep your usual routine as closely as you can: the same wind-down, the same sound, roughly the same bedtime.
  • Go easy on yourself. A rough first night is normal and usually passes by the second. If you're also crossing time zones, our guide to holiday travel and sleep covers jet lag.
