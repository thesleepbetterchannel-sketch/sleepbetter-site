Using Sound to Block Out Noisy Neighbors, Traffic and City Noise

Night noise does more than annoy you. Here's what the World Health Organization says about it, how masking sound helps, and when earplugs or a few room changes will work better.

November 6, 2026 · 3 min read

Few things are more frustrating than lying awake because of someone else's noise: the upstairs neighbor's footsteps, a TV through the wall, buses and sirens at 2 a.m. If that's your bedroom, you're far from alone, and there's a lot you can do about it.

Night noise is a real health issue

The World Health Organization takes night noise seriously. Its night noise guidelines for Europe set a target that average outdoor noise at night should stay under 40 decibels, roughly a quiet residential street. Its experts concluded there is strong evidence that night noise raises heart rate and causes arousals, sleep-stage changes and awakenings.

Much of this happens without you knowing. Sleep-lab research shows that traffic noises that don't wake you can still briefly raise your heart rate. So even if you think you're sleeping through it, noise may be costing you some quality.

How masking sound helps

Masking works by raising the background level of your room so that sudden noises stand out less. It's the jump from quiet to loud that disturbs sleep, so a steady sound that narrows that jump can help a lot.

Match the sound to the noise:

  • Low rumbles (traffic hum, footsteps, bass from next door, a furnace or train): brown noise or heavy rain, which carry plenty of low-frequency energy.
  • Voices, TV chatter, barking: white noise, a fan sound or bright rain, which cover the middle and upper range where speech sits.
  • A mix of everything: rain is a strong all-rounder because it covers a wide spread of pitches.

Keep the volume as low as will do the job. The NIDCD considers 70 decibels and below safe for long listening, and you'll usually need far less than that to take the edge off.

Don't skip earplugs

Here's something many sleep-sound sites won't tell you. In a 2026 University of Pennsylvania sleep-lab study, foam earplugs protected sleep from simulated traffic noise far better than pink noise did. Earplugs recovered most of the deep sleep that noise had taken away, while pink noise helped only a little and reduced REM sleep.

If outside noise is your main problem, try earplugs first, or combine them with a low background sound. Foam earplugs are cheap, and it's worth trying a few shapes to find a comfortable fit.

Small changes to the room

  • Move the bed away from a shared wall or a street-facing window if you can.
  • Add soft surfaces. Heavy curtains, a rug and a padded headboard absorb some sound.
  • Seal the gaps. Weatherstripping around doors and windows blocks more noise than you'd expect.
  • Run the fan or sound on your side of the room, between you and the noise.

When to talk to someone

If noise from a neighbor is constant and late, a friendly conversation often fixes more than any gadget. And if you've sorted the noise but still sleep badly, it may be worth talking to your doctor about what else is going on.
