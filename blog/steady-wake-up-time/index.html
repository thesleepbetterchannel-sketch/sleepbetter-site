Why a Steady Wake-Up Time Matters More Than Your Bedtime

A study of more than 60,000 people found that sleeping on a regular schedule predicted health better than how long they slept. Here's why consistency matters, and why it's easiest to start in the morning.

November 6, 2026 · 3 min read

Most sleep advice is about getting more sleep. A growing body of research suggests that getting regular sleep may matter just as much, and the easiest place to start is your alarm clock.

The 60,000-person study

In a 2024 study published in Sleep, researchers used more than 10 million hours of wrist-tracker data from about 61,000 UK Biobank participants to measure how regular each person's sleep was. They then followed participants for around six years.

People with the most irregular sleep had the highest risk of dying during the study. Compared with the least regular group, people in the more regular groups had a 20 to 48 percent lower risk of death from any cause, and lower risks of dying from cancer and from heart and metabolic disease. The results held after adjusting for age, sex, lifestyle and health factors.

The striking part: sleep regularity was a stronger predictor than sleep duration.

Read it carefully

This was an observational study, so it shows a link, not proof that irregular sleep causes harm. An irregular schedule might partly be a sign of poor health rather than its cause, and an editorial on the study noted that UK Biobank participants aren't very diverse. Still, it adds to a lot of evidence that your body clock prefers a predictable rhythm.

Why start with wake-up time

You can't make yourself fall asleep at a set time, but you can get up at one. A fixed wake-up time does several jobs at once:

  • It anchors your body clock. Morning light at the same time each day tells your internal clock when the day begins.
  • It sets up the next night. Waking at the same time builds a predictable amount of sleep pressure by evening, so you're sleepy at roughly the same time.
  • It's the core of insomnia treatment. Waking at the same time every morning is one of the standard instructions in stimulus control, a key part of the most effective therapy for chronic insomnia.

The CDC's advice is the same: go to bed and get up at the same time each day, including weekends.

How to do it

  1. Pick a wake-up time you can keep seven days a week. Be realistic.
  2. Count back seven to eight hours for a target bedtime, then go to bed when you feel sleepy, not before.
  3. Get light soon after you wake. Open the curtains or step outside for a few minutes.
  4. Keep weekends close. Try to stay within about an hour of your weekday time. A big lie-in on Saturday is like flying a couple of time zones west and back.
  5. After a bad night, still get up on time. It's tempting to sleep in, but holding your wake time is what gets you back on track.
