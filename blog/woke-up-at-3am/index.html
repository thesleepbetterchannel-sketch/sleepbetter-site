Woke Up at 3 a.m.? How to Get Back to Sleep

Waking in the night is normal. Lying there for an hour getting frustrated isn't something you have to live with. Here's what sleep therapists actually recommend.

November 6, 2026 · 3 min read

First, some reassurance: waking briefly during the night is normal. Everyone surfaces a few times as they move between sleep stages, and most of us don't remember it. The problem isn't waking up. It's being stuck awake, watching the clock and getting more frustrated by the minute.

Here's what sleep therapists recommend, drawn from the techniques used in cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), the first-line treatment recommended by the American College of Physicians for chronic insomnia.

1. Don't look at the clock

Checking the time almost always makes things worse. You start doing math ("if I fall asleep now I'll get four hours"), and that worry wakes you up even more. Turn the clock to face the wall and leave your phone across the room.

2. Stay calm and stay put, at first

Keep the lights off, breathe slowly, and let yourself rest. If you use a sleep sound, a soft steady track can give your mind something gentle to settle on. Often that's enough, and you'll drift off without noticing.

3. If you're still awake after about 20 minutes, get up

This is the part most people resist, but it's one of the most effective tools there is. Sleep therapists call it stimulus control. The University of Pennsylvania's CBT-I program advises that if you've been awake for roughly 15 to 20 minutes, you should get up, go to another room and do something quiet and relaxing, then return to bed only when you feel sleepy. It also says to get up right away if you start to feel frustrated or anxious.

Don't time it with a clock. Just estimate.

Why does it work? Lying awake in bed night after night teaches your brain that bed is a place for being awake and worrying. Getting up breaks that link, so the bed goes back to meaning sleep.

4. What to do while you're up

Keep it calm and dim:

  • Read something light from a paper book.
  • Listen to quiet music, a gentle audiobook or your sleep sound.
  • Sit in a comfortable chair with a blanket.

Avoid bright screens, work, email and snacks. If it helps, set up a "night spot" in advance with a lamp, a book and a blanket, so getting up feels easy.

5. In the morning, get up at your usual time

Even after a rough night, resist sleeping in or taking a long nap. Holding your normal wake-up time builds up the sleep pressure that helps you sleep better the next night.

Common 3 a.m. culprits

  • Alcohol. It often causes disrupted sleep in the second half of the night, right when it wears off.
  • A warm room. Your body temperature naturally rises toward morning. A cooler bedroom helps.
  • Noise or light. A passing car, a partner getting up, or early dawn through thin curtains.
  • Worry. Keep a notepad by the bed to jot thoughts down and deal with them in the morning.

When it's more than a rough patch

If you're waking and struggling to get back to sleep at least three nights a week for three months or more, and it's affecting your days, that matches the definition of chronic insomnia. It's very treatable, and CBT-I is available in person, online, by phone and through self-help books. Talk to your doctor about it.
